👋 Hello there! Welcome to class.

Today, we are going to explore something that is the absolute "skeleton" of nursing. Without this, nursing would just be guessing! 🧩

We are talking about the Nursing Process.

Let's start with a definition...

Imagine you want to build a LEGO castle. You don't just throw bricks at the floor, right? No! You need a plan. 🏗️

The Nursing Process is exactly that—a systematic, rational method of planning and providing individualized nursing care.

Its purpose is very clear: To identify a client’s health status (how are they doing?), find their actual or potential health problems, make a plan to fix those problems, and then actually deliver the specific nursing care needed. 💊✨

And remember, a "client" isn't just one person. It could be an individual, a family, a whole community, or a group.

A little bit of history...

Who came up with this brilliant idea? 🤔

A lady named Hall originated the term "nursing process" way back in 1955. That’s a long time ago!

Later on, other pioneers like Johnson (1959), Orlando (1961), and Wiedenbach (1963) were among the first to use it to describe the phases of nursing practice. 📚

Since then, various nurses have described the process of nursing and organized the phases in different ways, but the core idea remains the same.

Now, let’s look at its Characteristics...

The nursing process isn't just a boring list. It has a special "personality" or characteristics that let nurses handle a patient's changing health. 🌟

Let's break them down simply:

1️⃣ Cyclic and Dynamic: Imagine a bicycle wheel. 🚲 Data from one phase feeds into the next. Findings from the evaluation phase feed back into assessment. It is a regularly repeated event (a cycle) that is continuously changing (dynamic) rather than staying static (stuck in one place).

2️⃣ Client Centered: This is huge! The nurse organizes the plan of care according to the client's problems, not just what the nurse feels like doing. We check the client's habits and routines to make the care plan fit them perfectly. 👔

3️⃣ Problem Solving: It’s basically the scientific method (problem solving) adapted for nurses. It runs parallel to the medical model (what doctors use), but it is separate.

4️⃣ Decision Making: Nurses are smart! 🧠 Decision making happens in every single phase. We aren't robots bound by standard responses; we use creativity, skills, and knowledge to help clients.

5️⃣ Interpersonal and Collaborative: We have to talk to people! It requires direct communication with clients and families. Plus, we collaborate (work together) with the whole health care team. 🤝

6️⃣ Universal Applicability: This means it works everywhere—hospitals, clinics, homes—and with clients of all ages. 🌍

Okay, let's now move on to...

The Phases of the Nursing Process. This is the step-by-step recipe.

Depending on who you ask, there are either 5 or 6 phases:

🔹 The ANA (2010) says 6 phases: Assessment, Diagnosis, Outcomes Identification, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation.

🔹 The NCLEX (your big exam!) uses 5 phases: Assessment, Analysis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation.

Most people use the classic 5 steps: Assessing, Diagnosing, Planning, Implementing, and Evaluating.

⚠️ Important Note: These phases overlap! They aren't separate boxes. For example, while you are giving medicine (Implementation), you are also checking if the patient looks pale (Assessment). It's all connected!

If you mess up the first step (Assessment), your Diagnosis will be wrong, and your Plan will fail. It's like buttoning a shirt—if you miss the first button, the whole shirt is crooked! 👔❌

Let's dive deep into Step 1: Assessment

🕵️‍♀️ Assessment is the systematic and continuous collection, organization, validation, and documentation of data.

Think of yourself as a detective. You are gathering clues. This happens continuously. Even during evaluation, you are assessing to see if your plan worked.

There are four types of assessments you should know:

1. Initial Nursing Assessment: The very first check-up.

2. Problem Focused Assessment: Zooming in on one specific issue (like a hurt leg).

3. Emergency Assessment: Crisis mode! 🚑

4. Time-lapsed Reassessment: Checking in after some time has passed.

Why do we assess?

To establish a database about the client's physical, emotional, and social health. We want to find their strengths (like a supportive family or good coping skills) and their problems.

This is also where we become friends with the client (Therapeutic Relationship) and let them talk about their goals. 🗣️

The essential elements here are: Collection, Verification, Organization, Interpretation, and Documentation.

Next, we look at Step 2: Nursing Diagnosis

Once you have the clues (data), you have to figure out what they mean. 🧐

The Nursing Diagnosis is a clinical judgment about a person's response to health conditions. It’s different from a medical diagnosis (which identifies a disease). A nursing diagnosis identifies how the patient is responding to that disease.

We use this to communicate with other nurses. It gives us a common language!

A quick history lesson...

The term has been around since the 1950s (thanks, Fry, 1953!). But it took a while to catch on. In 1973, nurses met to classify these diagnoses, and by 1982, the group was renamed NANDA (North American Nursing Diagnosis Association). They are the "bosses" of nursing diagnosis! 📘

Why is this important?

Professionalism: Having our own unique knowledge makes nursing a true profession. 🎓

Communication: It creates a "mutual vocabulary" so nurses, doctors, and computers can all understand the patient's needs clearly.

Holistic Care: The NANDA list helps us treat the whole person, not just the body part that hurts.

Let's break down the Components...

A nursing diagnosis is made of pieces:

🔸 Label: The name of the diagnosis (e.g., "Anxiety").

🔸 Definition: Describes what it is.

🔸 Defining Characteristics: The clues you see (signs and symptoms).

🔸 Risk Factors: Things that make a problem more likely to happen.

🔸 Related Factors: What is causing or linked to the problem.

How do we write it? The Statements.

There are two main ways to write a diagnosis. Think of this as grammar for nurses. ✍️

1. The Two-Part Statement (NANDA Style)

This is short and sweet. It includes:

🅰️ Problem Statement (Label): The issue (e.g., Disturbed Body Image).

🅱️ Etiology: The cause or related factor.

We join them with the words "Related To" (RT).

Example: "Disturbed body image RT loss of left lower extremity."

We can also add modifiers like "Ineffective," "Impaired," or "Risk for" to make it specific.

At this point, it's important to consider...

2. The Three-Part Statement

Some nurses like to be extra detailed! They add a third part. This format is:

🅰️ Label + 🅱️ Etiology + 🆎 Defining Characteristics (Signs & Symptoms).

We connect the third part using "As Evidenced By" (AEB).

So it looks like: Problem... caused by... Etiology... shown by... Signs/Symptoms.

NANDA provides lists of these defining characteristics to help us verify our diagnosis. While some nurses love the 3-part statement for its detail, others prefer the 2-part statement for being quick and precise.

Let's now dive into...

The different Categories of Nursing Diagnoses. Think of these as different flavors of health statuses. 🍦

1️⃣ Actual Diagnoses: These are problems that are happening right now. They are already here!
Example: Fluid overload or Anxiety. The rain is already falling. 🌧️

2️⃣ Risk Diagnoses: Think of this as a warning sign! ⚠️ The problem hasn't happened yet, but the client is "vulnerable."
Example: Risk for poisoning (maybe a baby is crawling near cleaning supplies). It’s cloudy, but not raining yet!

3️⃣ Health Promotion Diagnoses: This identifies behaviors that show a desire to increase well-being.

4️⃣ Wellness Diagnoses: This is when a client is already healthy but wants to get even better.
Note: These are special! They don't use "Related To." We just say "Readiness for enhanced..."
Example: Readiness for enhanced community coping. 💪

Now that we understand this, let's talk about Step 3...

Planning and Outcome Identification. 📝

Imagine planning a birthday party. You can't just hope a cake appears. You need a plan! The ANA (2004) says planning is essential for competent nursing.

After we find the problems (Diagnosis), we make a plan of action. This improves communication so all nurses know what to do!

There are four critical elements to Planning:

🔸 Establishing priorities (What do we do first?)

🔸 Setting goals and expected outcomes

🔸 Planning nursing interventions

🔸 Documenting nursing care

First: Establishing Priorities

We can't do everything at once! We have to rank the diagnoses. Which one is life-threatening? Which one is psychological?

💡 Pro Tip: Do this WITH the client! When you share decision-making power, the client feels motivated and in control. "Teamwork makes the dream work!"

Next: Goals and Expected Outcomes

What is the difference? 🤔

🎯 Goal: A broad, big-picture aim. Example: "The client will walk again."

📋 Expected Outcome: A detailed, specific step. Example: "The client will walk 10 feet by Tuesday."

We have Short-term goals (hours or days) and Long-term goals (weeks or months). Be careful! If you get the cause of the problem wrong, your goal won't be met.

Outcomes must be: Realistic, Mutually desired (client wants it too!), and Attainable in a specific time.

Then: Planning Interventions

Now we choose our weapons! ⚔️ A Nursing Intervention is an action the nurse performs to help the client reach those goals.

We use science and critical thinking to pick the best ones. If one doesn't work, we need backup plans!

Example: If the diagnosis is "Pain related to heart problems," the intervention is "Help the client rest to save energy." Simple, right?

Next, we'll look at Step 4: Implementation

Action time! 🎬 Implementation is the "doing" phase. We execute the plan we just made.

To do this well, you need three types of skills:

🧠 Cognitive Skills (The Brain): You need to understand the "why" behind what you are doing. You need to analyze data and use your knowledge.

✋ Psychomotor Skills (The Hands): You need to be good with your hands! This means knowing how to use equipment, give injections, or move a patient safely.

🗣️ Interpersonal Skills (The Voice): You need to communicate! This builds a therapeutic relationship with the patient and helps you work with the whole medical team.

And finally, Step 5: Evaluation

Did it work? 🤷‍♂️ Evaluation is where we see if the goals were met, partially met, or not met at all.

This isn't just the end; it's also a new beginning! If the plan didn't work, we go back to the start and figure out why. It ensures quality care and accountability.

Evaluation Components: It is a fluid process. It depends on all the other steps. If your assessment was wrong, your evaluation will show failure.

Techniques: Use your senses! 👀👂 What do you see? What do you hear? Are there subtle changes in the patient's behavior? You have to be a sharp observer!

And that brings us to the end of our journey through the Nursing Process! 🏁
